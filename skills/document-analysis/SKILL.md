# Document Analysis

Step 1. Check what you actually have. A file named in the message may not have been attached, or only some passages of it may be in your context. Say which it is: the whole text, selected excerpts, or nothing. Never describe a document you cannot see, and never fill in what you assume it says.

Step 2. Take inventory before analysing: type of document, title, author and date if shown, number of pages or sections, headings, tables, figures, and anything that looks unreadable (scanned pages, garbled characters, missing images). Report gaps, for example "tables 2 and 3 came through as unreadable text".

Step 3. Work out what the user wants: a summary, specific facts, a comparison, a critique, or a decision. If it is unclear and matters, ask one short question. Otherwise state your reading of the task in one line and continue.

Step 4. For long documents, go through them section by section. Write a one or two line note per section, then combine the notes. Do not summarize from the beginning and the end only. If only excerpts are available, say the answer covers those excerpts, and ask for the missing parts or suggest attaching them again.

Step 5. Answer from the document. Give the page, section or heading for each important claim so the user can check it. Keep quotes short and exact, and put them in quotation marks. Do not mix your own knowledge into the answer unless you label it clearly as outside knowledge.

Step 6. Numbers and tables: copy figures exactly with units and dates. Recompute totals, percentages and differences with code or step by step, and flag any number in the document that does not add up. Say when a table lost its structure and you had to guess which value belongs to which column.

Step 7. Look for what a careful reader would notice: contradictions between sections, definitions that change, dates and amounts that disagree, missing information, vague wording, deadlines, obligations, exceptions and conditions (for contracts and policies), method, sample size and limits (for studies).

Step 8. If the answer is not in the document, write "not stated in the document". If two passages conflict, show both and say which one you would trust and why.

Step 9. Format the result: the direct answer first, then supporting points with page or section references, then caveats. Keep summaries well under the length of the original. For legal, medical or financial documents, explain what the text says and add one line that this is not professional advice.

Step 10. Spreadsheets and CSV files: describe the sheets, columns, row counts and any empty or odd cells first, then follow the Data Analysis skill if it is available.
